\section{Overview}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The released network did not change.} Its weights and scores are as before.
\item \textbf{The closed form changed} from 24 to \val{cf.n_parameters} parameters and was scored once on the test split
  (Section~\ref{sec:cf}).
\item \textbf{The output-family tests are new} (Section~\ref{sec:fam}). The two-exponent head loses a little to the best
  mixture on validation and beats it on the sticky split. Fitted freely, the two exponents do not follow the single-$\nu$ tie.
\item \textbf{The STARLING comparison is new} (Section~\ref{sec:starling}). The network and STARLING agree closely on shape, mostly on
  the shape that proteins share, and agree less on what separates one protein from another.
\end{itemize}

\section{The closed form: 24 to \texorpdfstring{\val{cf.n_parameters}}{21} parameters}\label{sec:cf}

\paragraph{What changed.} The form now has \val{cf.n_coefficients} coefficients and two threshold constants.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\ln S_m$ goes from 11 to 9 coefficients: $s_+\ln N$ and $h\ln N$ are removed.
\item $u$ keeps 6 coefficients: $\ln N$ is removed and $s_+/N$ is added.
\item $\ln A$ goes from 5 to 4: $s_+$ is removed.
\item The CE threshold slope was reselected on the new form and stays at $c_1=\val{cf.c1}$, with $c_0=\val{cf.c0}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why.} The aim was to remove a hard edge in the $\nu$-versus-size plots at short chains with an excess of like charge, without
giving up parameter economy. Adding $s_+/N$ to $u$ did that, but it failed on the contact-energy set. Removing $s_+$ from $\ln A$ is what
fixed that set (NLL change from $+0.024$ to $-0.0098$ against the published form). The next removal, $s_+$ from $u$, failed at
$+2.1$ standard errors, so the search stopped there. Details are in \texttt{experiments/u\_terms/README.md}.

\section{Output families}\label{sec:fam}

\subsection{Question and design}
Does the two-exponent form carry the shape of the distribution on the network itself? The network was retrained with only its output head changed:
the same inputs, NLL only, 40 epochs, last epoch, seeds 42--44. It was trained on the cluster split's training set and scored on validation and on the
length and sticky extrapolation splits. The test split was not read, and \texttt{model/} was not touched. Validation has informed this project's
choices before, so this comparison is descriptive and did not select the released model. Table~\ref{tab:fam} lists the heads, in order of decreasing
freedom. The first row is the earlier recipe (moment penalties and checkpoint selection on validation; two seeds on the extrapolation splits).

\paragraph{What is and is not the head.} Most of the gain over the earlier $K=3$ model comes from its recipe, not from the family. On the sticky split, mean $W_1$
falls from \pct{fam.run20_512x3.sticky.w1_mean_pct} to \pct{fam.mix3_512x3.sticky.w1_mean_pct} by moving to NLL-only training alone, and the two-exponent head then takes it to
\pct{fam.law2_512x2.sticky.w1_mean_pct}. At two parameters per protein the family does decide the result: the released head beats the log-normal ($K=1$) by
\val{fam.mix1_512x2.val.dnll_1e3}${}\pm{}$\val{fam.mix1_512x2.val.dnll_se_1e3}$\times10^{-3}$ nats per frame on validation.

\paragraph{Limitation.} Every head uses the same anchor slope, fitted on the whole training split, so it has seen the length split's evaluation proteins. This applies to all
heads equally.

\subsection{Is the physics of the tie recovered?}
Here $\nu_\delta$ and $\nu_g$ are the exponents implied by $\delta=1/(1-\nu_\delta)$ and $g=3/(3\nu_g-1)$. One $\nu$ means $\nu_\delta=\nu_g$, and so
$g=3\delta/(2\delta-3)$.

\paragraph{Within the tied form, yes.} Freeing $A$ barely moves $\nu$: free-$A$ against published $\nu$ has $R^2=\val{fam.exp.val.freeA_nu_r2}$ on validation (mean absolute difference
\val{fam.exp.val.freeA_nu_abs}), \val{fam.exp.length.freeA_nu_r2} on length and \val{fam.exp.sticky.freeA_nu_r2} on sticky. The free $\ln A$ follows the amplitude law with
$R^2=\val{fam.exp.val.lnA_law_r2}$ on validation, weaker on length (\val{fam.exp.length.lnA_law_r2}), sticky (\val{fam.exp.sticky.lnA_law_r2}) and direct MD fits
(\val{fam.exp.md.law_on_tied_r2}). Freeing $A$ changes validation NLL by \val{fam.freeA_512x2.val.dnll_1e3}${}\pm{}$\val{fam.freeA_512x2.val.dnll_se_1e3}$\times10^{-3}$. The law is an
empirical fit, so this shows that it works, not that theory predicted it.

\paragraph{Between the two exponents, no.} The network and direct fits to the frames, with no network, agree (Table~\ref{tab:exp}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{It is not a network artefact.} On the validation proteins MD gives $\nu_\delta=\val{fam.exp.md.val_md_nu_delta}$ and $\nu_g=\val{fam.exp.md.val_md_nu_g}$. Protein by protein the network follows MD only moderately
  (correlation \val{fam.exp.md.net_corr_nu_delta} for $\nu_\delta$, \val{fam.exp.md.net_corr_nu_g} for $\nu_g$, \val{fam.exp.md.net_corr_gap} for the gap). The network's gap is positive on every validation protein, which
  overstates how uniform it is: in MD $\nu_\delta>\nu_g$ on \val{fam.exp.md.frac_nu_delta_above}\% of proteins.
\item \textbf{It is above noise.} Replicate simulations differ in the gap by a median of \val{fam.exp.md.replicate_gap_abs_diff} (\val{fam.exp.md.replicate_pairs} pairs, \val{fam.exp.md.replicate_sequences} sequences), and the gap is positive in both
  members of \val{fam.exp.md.replicate_both_pos_pct}\% of pairs. Held-out NLL favours independent exponents on \val{fam.exp.md.frac_heldout_better}\% of proteins, though the median gain is small:
  \val{fam.exp.md.dnll_median_1e3}$\times10^{-3}$ nats per frame.
\item \textbf{It shrinks with length and does not reach zero.} The gap is \val{fam.exp.md.gap_N_le50} at $N\leq50$, \val{fam.exp.md.gap_N_50_100} at $50<N\leq100$, \val{fam.exp.md.gap_N_100_200} at $100<N\leq200$,
  \val{fam.exp.md.gap_N_200_300} at $200<N\leq300$ and \val{fam.exp.md.gap_N_gt300} above. Above $N=300$, $\nu_\delta=\val{fam.exp.md.nu_delta_N_gt300}$, $\nu_g=\val{fam.exp.md.nu_g_N_gt300}$ and the tied $\nu$ is \val{fam.exp.md.nu_tied_N_gt300}.
  It is not an effect of the padded short chains: on the \val{fam.exp.md.unpadded_n} proteins without padded frames the gap is \val{fam.exp.md.unpadded_gap}, positive on \val{fam.exp.md.unpadded_frac_gap_pct}\%.
\item \textbf{It costs little.} The median $W_1$ between the independent-exponent and released densities is \pct{fam.exp.val.w1_free4_vs_pub_median_pct} of $\mRg$, against \pct{fam.exp.val.w1_seed_to_seed_median_pct} between seeds of the released head. The visible cost is
  in skewness and kurtosis (Table~\ref{tab:fam}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What this does not test.} The Victor--Lhuillier relation concerns asymptotic tails and long chains. The frames span
$s\approx\val{fam.exp.md.s_q01}$--\val{fam.exp.md.s_q999} (0.1st to 99.9th percentile, median protein), so these are effective exponents of the central distribution. $\delta$, $g$ and $A$ also trade off against each other.
This does not refute the relation. It means the data do not recover it.

\paragraph{Wording.} The two-exponent form captures the distribution with far fewer parameters than a free mixture, and a single effective $\nu$ is a practical approximation that costs little. It is not a recovered scaling relation.

\subsection{The exponent, and why a poor \texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu} correlation can coexist with a small \texorpdfstring{$W_1$}{W1}}
The exponent does test asymmetry, and it agrees less. Fitted to the STARLING ensembles with $\ln A$ free (\texttt{experiments/starling/fit\_nu.py}, the fit \texttt{fig24} uses for MD), $\nu$ correlates with the network's at $\rho=\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho}$
(\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_ci.0}--\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_ci.1}), against \val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_hps1} for the same fit to HPS1. Most of this is the \val{fig.fig26.n_a1} A1-LCD variants: median $\nu$ \val{fig.fig26.nu.a1_star} against \val{fig.fig26.nu.a1_net}, and skewness \val{fig.fig26.nu.a1_skew_star} against \val{fig.fig26.nu.a1_skew_net}. Without them
$\rho=\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_rest}$ (\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_rest_ci.0}--\val{fig.fig26.nu.rho_rest_ci.1}).

The checks below are in \texttt{experiments/starling/explore/}; their numbers are literal, not macros.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Not a normalization error.} Fits to raw and normalized samples give $\nu$ differing by at most $3\times10^{-8}$, as they must, since rescaling only moves $\ln S_m$.
\item \textbf{Not a poor fit.} The fitted curves match the samples with a median $W_1$ of 0.42\%, the noise floor.
\item \textbf{Not the choice of free $A$.} With $A$ from the network's law $\rho=0.05$, and with $A$ fixed at the network's value $\rho=-0.03$.
\item \textbf{The two measures answer different questions.} Between pairs of the network's own predictions, a $\nu$ difference of 0.03--0.06 corresponds to a median shape $W_1$ of 0.61\%, 0.06--0.10 to 1.09\%, and 0.10--0.20 to 2.65\%. So the mean $\nu$ difference from STARLING, 0.051, corresponds to a small $W_1$
  but is larger than the network's spread in $\nu$ across proteins (standard deviation 0.037, $N\geq20$). In the STARLING fits $\nu$ is mostly a readout of skewness ($\rho=-0.91$), and $W_1$ is little affected by the tail.
\item \textbf{A1-LCD is a real difference.} Forcing the network's $\nu$ onto these ensembles is strongly rejected (median likelihood-ratio statistic 64, with 3.84 the 5\% cutoff). These are also the proteins whose size differs most between the force fields, 24 of the 26 largest offsets. HPS1 over-expands some of them: A1\_LCD\_10R has a SAXS $\Rg$ of 2.67~nm, against 3.32 in HPS1 and 2.58 in STARLING. So STARLING's shape might be the better one for this family, but testing that needs Mpipi-GG distributions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Choosing the panels}
Three designs were tried and dropped before the final one.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Size against shape.} The first version compared the two force fields by size. It was dropped because comparing force fields by size says nothing about shape, which is the point.
\item \textbf{$\nu$ scatter.} Replaced by the tail probabilities; its result stays in the text above.
\item \textbf{Bowley skewness.} $(q_{75}+q_{25}-2q_{50})/(q_{75}-q_{25})$ was tried as an asymmetry measure and was noisier than $\nu$ relative to its signal. The median sampling error of 0.032 exceeds the network's spread of 0.016, and noise limits $\rho$ to 0.33. The observed $\rho$ was $-0.01$ against STARLING and 0.29 against HPS1. Its 87\% inside the band reflects a band of $\pm0.062$ wider than nearly all the data, not agreement.
\end{itemize}
Choosing a panel after seeing that $\nu$ looked poor is a selection, which is why the $\nu$ result remains in the text.

The first version used fixed thresholds of 0.8 and 1.3 times $\mRg$, chosen before the panels were computed. On 2026-11-01 they were replaced by $t_c$ and $t_e$, the length-matched 5th and 95th percentiles of a typical training chain (\texttt{proteome/populations.py}). That choice was made after these panels had been seen, using the validation proteins and the benchmark, without the test split. The fixed thresholds gave a network--HPS1 $\rho$ of 0.69 for the compact probability, but 0.44 with length removed, because the probability rises with $N$. The depth trades prediction against information: on the benchmark, the compact-tail $\rho$ with length removed is 0.13 at the 5th percentile and 0.42 at the 15th, while the 15th percentile is nearly a measure of spread in MD ($\rho=0.90$, against 0.79). On the larger validation set the depth makes no difference among low-charge chains (0.52 and 0.49), so the 5th percentile was kept. Those numbers are literal, not macros.

\subsection{What can be claimed}
The common shape is robust to the choice of these two force fields; a protein's departure from it is not shown to be. Three limits apply.
\begin{itemize}
\item Normalizing by size does not remove the force field entirely, as A1-LCD shows.
\item STARLING stands in for Mpipi-GG without a direct test, because no Mpipi-GG distributions are available.
\item Both force fields are coarse-grained with implicit solvent and SAXS gives only a mean, so this tests agreement between models, not the experimental distribution. ``Real'' is not supported.
\end{itemize}
